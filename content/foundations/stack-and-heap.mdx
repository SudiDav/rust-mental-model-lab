# Stack and Heap

You have seen where a program keeps its data. Now let’s watch that data live a little: create a name, send it to another function, and see what gets cleaned up when the function finishes.

You control the pace. Step forward, inspect a box, or rewind until the relationship makes sense. At two points, the lab will pause for your prediction. Getting one wrong is a chance to look again—you can always retry.

<Concept>A stack frame belongs to a function call and holds its local values in this model. A heap allocation holds dynamic data, such as the UTF-8 bytes in a String. Stack and heap are regions of a program’s memory, not separate physical devices.</Concept>

## Meet the owner and the bytes

Think of a `String` as a small descriptor and an allocation it owns. The descriptor has a **pointer** to the bytes, a **length** saying how many bytes are in use, and a **capacity** saying how much space is available. We will draw the descriptor in a stack frame and the bytes on the heap.

The example uses two functions. `main` creates the name, and `show` receives it as a parameter called `message`. Passing this String transfers ownership: the responsibility for eventually cleaning it up. You will explore ownership more deeply in a later lesson; here, watch where the data goes.

## What should stick?

- Calling a function adds a frame; returning removes that function’s frame first.
- Moving the String changes its owner without cloning the heap bytes.
- When the owner goes out of scope, the String is dropped and its allocation is released.
- The number `count` remains available in `main` while the other function runs.

<UnderTheHood>This is a deterministic walkthrough of the displayed program, not a general Rust interpreter. The address 0xA120 and the placement and sizes of boxes are illustrative. Rust does not guarantee that every local is physically on the stack: compiler optimizations may use registers or eliminate storage. The String’s field order is not shown as a guaranteed layout. Capacity is at least the byte length; allocator details can vary. Deallocation releases ownership of storage but does not promise to erase its old bytes. The “moved” ghost is a teaching aid, not a runtime flag.</UnderTheHood>

For your reflection, explain why the bytes stay in place when `name` becomes `message`, and why they are released when `show` returns.
